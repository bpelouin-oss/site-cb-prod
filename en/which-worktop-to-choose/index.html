Guiding your client towards the right worktop

Which worktop should you recommend when every material promises beauty and durability? The right answer depends on how your client will really use it: how hard the kitchen works, tolerance for upkeep, the look they want and their budget. This guide and our comparison help you steer the conversation before sending us the project.

For a hard-working kitchen

If your client cooks every day, moves pans straight from the hob to the worktop and uses the island as a desk or family table, steer them towards a forgiving material.

Ceramic, such as Dekton®, The Size or Infinity, scores highest in our comparison: it resists scratches, heat and stains, and its water absorption is close to zero. Granite and quartzite, very hard natural stones, also cope very well with heat and knocks, with slightly more careful upkeep. The treated Sensa versions reduce that need.

Quartz remains an excellent family surface, non-porous and easy to clean, but it tolerates direct heat less well: warn your client that a trivet must become second nature. Marble and Corian® are still possible, provided they accept that the surface will develop a patina over time.

Balancing budget and look

The price of a worktop does not depend on the material alone. The range and colour, thickness, finish, total area, number of cut-outs and slab yield all weigh just as much in the price.

A few options to put to your client to keep the budget under control without giving up on the look:

  • keep the most striking material for the island or splashback, and choose a quieter surface for the main run;
  • look at the thicknesses available in each range;
  • compare two families with a similar look, for example a natural stone and a quartz that echoes it;
  • limit complex cut-outs when they add no real convenience.

Tell us the target budget in your enquiry: we can price several consistent alternatives rather than a single quote that is hard to adjust, and optimise the cutting plan to reduce offcuts.

The marble look, without marble's constraints

Marble wins people over with its veining, but it is a softer stone, sensitive to acids such as lemon juice or vinegar. In the kitchen, several materials offer a similar look with fewer precautions, and it is often the question your clients put to you.

Quartzite is the first option: a natural stone harder than marble, it echoes its veining and stands up better to acids and stains. The Quartzite Sensa collection adds a factory-applied stain protection.

Large-format ceramics faithfully reproduce the great marbles, with slabs that keep joints to a minimum on an island or splashback. Some veined quartz surfaces play the same card, with a non-porous surface.

If your client is set on real marble, Marble Texta receives a surface treatment that improves its resistance. And marble remains perfect in a bathroom or for a table.

Meeting a request for a more responsible choice

More and more end clients ask for a lower-impact material. Several references let you give them a concrete answer.

Minéralis, distributed by MStone Surfaces, offers the look of quartz with 85% recycled first-production glass and a low silica content, which also makes it safer to work for those who fabricate it. It withstands 180 °C and comes with a 20-year warranty.

Our recycled stones follow the same path. MStone recycled stone combines recycled minerals with plant-based eco-resins. Compac's version includes recycled fragments of quartz and marble. Ostrea makes use of crushed oyster and scallop shells, which create a speckled pattern with a pearly sheen.

Finally, remind them that a well-chosen, well-maintained worktop lasts for many years: durability remains the first responsible choice.

Your questions

Which worktop should I recommend to a client who wants the most heat-resistant?

Ceramic is the most tolerant: made from minerals sintered at very high temperatures, it takes a hot pan without damage. Granite and quartzite also resist very well. Quartz, Corian® and marble call for more precautions. To be safe, recommend a trivet on every surface, in line with the manufacturer's guidance.

Which material should I suggest for a bathroom vanity top?

A bathroom sees fewer knocks and less heat than a kitchen, which widens the choice. Marble comes into its own there. Corian® allows integrated basins with no visible joint and curved shapes. Quartz, ceramic and Minéralis also work very well, thanks to their non-porous, easy-clean surfaces.

What thickness should I recommend for a worktop?

It depends on the material, the style and the cabinet structure. A slim thickness gives a fine, contemporary look, very popular in ceramic. A thicker top asserts the presence of an island or a natural stone. Available thicknesses vary by range: tell us about the project and we will tell you which ones are suitable.
